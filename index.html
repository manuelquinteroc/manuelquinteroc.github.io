Hola, I'm Manuel.

I'm a fourth-year PhD student at MIT in the Laboratory for Information and Decision Systems (LIDS) and the Institute for Data, Systems, and Society (IDSS), advised by Professor Tamara Broderick.

My interests lie broadly in statistics and machine learning, particularly causal inference and interpretability. More recently, I've become especially interested in developing machine learning methods and using AI tools to improve healthcare, with applications to disease understanding, diagnosis, treatment, and clinical decision-making.

Before MIT, I did a master's in mathematical statistics at Cambridge (Part III), and before that I studied applied math and economics at ITAM in Mexico City, where I spent a few years running the analysis for field experiments on gender-based violence and misinformation.

PreprintarXiv · 2026

Do covariates explain why these groups differ? The choice of reference group can reverse conclusions in the Oaxaca–Blinder decomposition

Manuel Quintero, Advik Shreekumar, William T. Stephenson, Tamara Broderick

The Oaxaca–Blinder decomposition splits a gap between two groups into differences in covariates and differences in outcomes given covariates. It requires picking one group as the reference, and we show that choice can flip the substantive conclusion, even with flexible models like a pretrained transformer. Report both directions.

PreprintarXiv · 2025

Network and Risk Analysis of Surety Bonds

Tamara Broderick, Ali Jadbabaie, Vanessa Lin, Manuel Quintero, Arnab Sarker, Sean R. Sinclair α–β

Large construction projects link many contractors, so one failure can spread. We model the contractor network as a directed graph, extend the Friedkin–Johnsen model to simulate how failures propagate, and find about 2% higher exposure for an insurer once network effects are included.

Common functional decompositions can mis-attribute differences in outcomes between populations

Manuel Quintero, William Stephenson, Advik Shreekumar, Tamara Broderick

Extending the Kitagawa–Oaxaca–Blinder decomposition with nonlinear tools like functional ANOVA or accumulated local effects seems natural. We show these can blame outcome differences on factors that are identical in both populations, and characterize when that happens.
